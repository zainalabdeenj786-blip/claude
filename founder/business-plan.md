## The idea

- What it is: a UK online brand selling halal-certified bovine collagen powder, with a share of proceeds funding meals for families in need.
- Who it is for: UK Muslims (and other halal-conscious buyers) who want a collagen supplement for skin, hair, nails and joints but avoid non-halal gelatine and collagen.
- What it sells, at what price: RizqPure Halal Collagen, a 300g pouch (30 daily servings) of unflavoured grass-fed bovine collagen peptides with vitamin C and biotin. £18.00 one-off; Subscribe & Save 20% off (£14.40). Free standard UK delivery on orders over £30. 30-day hassle-free returns.

- Budget and constraints: already trading; the site shows "4.8/5 · 320+ verified reviews". Unit costs, monthly spend, marketing budget and team size not yet given by the founder (open questions).

## Summary

**RizqPure** sells halal grass-fed bovine collagen powder (10g a day, with vitamin C and biotin) online to UK Muslims, at £18 a pouch. It is already trading.

**Verdict: Not yet.** It isn't failing on money. It fails because buyers can't see the proof that the brand's whole promise rests on.

- **Margin:** each pouch leaves **£4.03 (24%)** after its own costs, at a blended £16.65. The £6 pouch cost is yours; postage, fees and marketing are still **estimates**. Today's £14.40 subscription leaves only **£1.78** a pouch.
- **Break-even:** **5 pouches a day**. At a plan of 20 a day, **year 1 makes £12,869** on £82,917 revenue.
- **Buyer panel (today's offer):** **1 of 20 buys (5%)**, below the 25% bar. 14 of the 19 who passed gave the same reason: *"halal-certified… by whom?"*

**Where the board and the panel agree:** the hidden certifier is the problem. All three board members said so ("for this buyer, the halal certificate is the product"), and so did 14 of 19 buyers. Today the product page says "Contact us and we will share our certification." Both also flagged the health claims ("stronger joints", "firmer skin") as unlikely to be allowed in GB as written.

**Where they disagree:** the meals-for-families pledge. One board member counts it as a real, cheap bonus, and another says it dilutes the message. In the panel, it was the reason the 1 buyer in round 1 bought and 2 of 12 in round 2. Keep it, but make it specific: a named partner, an amount per pouch, and a live total.

**What changes the result:** with the certifier named on the pouch, a £4.95 trial pack, free subscription delivery and a 60-day guarantee, the same 20 buyers went from **5% to 60%**. But every one of those 12 bought only the trial, so this shows the block is removable. It doesn't show the business is profitable. Competitors confirm the opening: only one halal collagen names its certifier on its product page (Beauvyn, £24.99, new), and the market's biggest complaint is subscription traps (23 reviews).

**Biggest risk:** marketing cost per pouch. At £6 instead of the estimated £3, year 1 **loses £2,071**. Close behind is the halal status being publicly questioned, which is the brand's only moat. Fixing the certifier is what lowers both risks.

**What you need to start:** about **£5,000** for the fixes (pack reprint with the certificate seal, a lab test, trial sachets, a claims review, a launch push). On these numbers it is earned back in month 8. Then a real test: **150 paid £4.95 trial pre-orders in 3 weeks from organic channels** (`launch.md`), before printing anything.

**The one thing to do this week:** confirm in writing which body certifies your collagen and what the certificate covers (the bovine source, the slaughter and whether it was stunned, the plant), and put it on the product page. If it's a body your buyers don't recognise, changing certifier comes before everything else in this plan.

_The panel is simulated buyers, and the numbers are projections from estimated costs. Real customers and real quotes confirm them. This is not financial, legal or tax advice._

## What the board said

**Vote: 3 fund if, 0 pass. Average score 5.0 / 10** (Offers 5, Monopoly 4, Product 6). Memos: `board/offers.md`, `board/monopoly.md`, `board/product.md`.

### Risks more than one member raised

1. **The halal certifier is not named on the page we read.** All three said this. For this buyer, the halal certificate is the product. If they can't check it, they have no reason to switch, and one public challenge could undo the brand.
2. **The health claims are probably not allowed in GB as written.** All three said this. "Firmer skin", "thicker hair" and "mobility" in 4-6 weeks are claimed for collagen, and no authorised claim exists for collagen itself. The allowed claims belong to the vitamin C and biotin. Rewriting the claims removes the copy that is doing the persuading today.
3. **The formula is a commodity, so there's no moat yet.** Offers and Monopoly said this. Any brand can buy a halal certificate. If a big collagen brand adds a halal line, only price and brand are left.
4. **Unit economics are unknown.** All three said this. Nobody knows the cost per pouch, the cost to win a customer, the margin after the 20% subscription discount, or how long subscribers stay.

### Where they disagreed

- **The charity element.** Offers counts it as a real, cheap bonus. Product says it is vague and dilutes the one message: make it specific and checkable, or cut it. The panel can settle this.
- **How much the market itself is the problem.** Monopoly scores lowest because there's no secret and no technology moat, only brand. Product scores highest because the one-product, one-format focus is right and the experience fits a daily habit.

### 6. The gap RizqPure can own

**"Halal you can check, and a subscription that respects you, at £15-18."**

The evidence:
- The largest complaint theme across the halal leaders is subscription billing (23 reviews). Reviewers read it as a breach of trust by a faith-branded company. ### 3. Halal certification: the most important supplier

The panel's top objection (14 of 19 passes) was "who certified it?" Buyers named bodies themselves, and asked whether stunned slaughter is accepted. UK bodies differ on exactly this:

- **HMC (Halal Monitoring Committee)**: prohibits all pre-slaughter stunning; hand slaughter by a Muslim slaughterman. ([halalhmc.org/about](https://halalhmc.org/about/))
- **HFA (Halal Food Authority)**: permits reversible electrical stunning if the animal is alive before the cut. ([comparison](https://halalspy.com/halal-knowledge/certification/hmc-vs-hfa/))
- Others active in the UK: Halal Certification Europe (HCE), UKIM, EHDA ([overview](https://halalspy.com/halal-knowledge/certification/halal-certifying-bodies-uk/)). Imported collagen is often certified abroad (for example JAKIM, MUI, IFANCA). Check whether your buyers recognise yours.

Actions:
1. Find out exactly which body certifies your collagen raw material and your packing plant, and what the certificate covers (source animal, slaughter, processing, packing).
2. If it is a body this audience distrusts, or it covers the plant but not the raw material, that is the first thing to fix in this whole pack.
3. Publish it: logo and number on the pouch, the certificate PDF on the product page, and an FAQ that answers "stunned or not?" plainly.
